# A Letter Keeps Its Audience

*By Patrick Amihood, Creator of Postmello*

Knowing who sent a message does not necessarily tell you where it came from.

A friend might forward something received from another friend, who received it from somewhere else. A picture, a joke, a claim or a video can travel through a chain of trusted people while its original source becomes increasingly difficult to see.

That matters when designing a space for correspondence between approved friends. Approving a person is a meaningful boundary. Forwarding can make that person a route for material from far outside it.

I wanted Postmello to give people room to make things for one another without building a channel for circulating whatever had reached the last inbox.

So Postmello has no forwarding action. A letter is made at a desk and addressed to friends. Receiving it does not provide a way to send that same letter on to someone new.

This became a much more interesting decision when we added Reply on Top.

Reply on Top lets someone write directly on the pages of a letter they received. The original marks stay fixed underneath the new ones. A friend might answer a question beside it, add to a drawing, or return a page with little comments in the margins.

It is an appealingly physical thing to do. It also means the reply contains someone else's letter. Suddenly, an innocent-looking choice of recipient becomes a choice about who gets to see that original page.

The answer in Postmello is deliberately narrow. Reply to the sender, or reply to the original group.

Imagine Alex writes to Bea and Cam. Bea can answer Alex privately. Or Bea can answer Alex and Cam together. Bea cannot use the reply to send Alex's letter to Drew.

For a larger group, the choice works the same way. The sender stays on the envelope, and the other original recipients are included together or removed together. Reply-all is offered only when everyone in that group is an approved friend of the person replying.

The same audience choice applies to a reply on fresh paper. Adding the original pages underneath does not open up a different set of destinations.

There are two reasons for keeping the group together. One is privacy: writing on someone's page should not quietly become permission to show it to new people. The other is inclusion within an existing exchange. A group reply does not offer a little recipient editor where everyone stays except one person.

A private answer to the writer is still useful. Sometimes a letter invites a personal response. That is an explicit, understandable choice, alongside answering everyone who was part of the original letter.

These boundaries do not control every possible use of a tablet. Someone could take a screenshot, repeat something in a new letter, or share it elsewhere. And the words a friend writes still matter, whatever interface carries them.

But the actions a product makes easy matter too. A forwarding control turns redistribution into a normal next step. Postmello gives that place to writing back.

The aim is to keep correspondence rooted in the people involved: a page someone made, the friends it was meant for, and an answer that respects that audience.
