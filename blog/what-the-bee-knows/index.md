# What the Bee Knows

*By Patrick Amihood, Creator of Postmello*

The courier bee has a job beyond carrying envelopes. The bee has to make sense of the moments when a digital letter behaves differently from a physical one.

Someone has finished writing, chosen an envelope, added the address and stamp, and dropped the letter into the outgoing mailbox. As far as the person at the desk is concerned, the work is done.

Then the internet gets involved.

Perhaps the connection has disappeared. Perhaps the letter has several richly decorated pages and needs a little longer to leave. Perhaps the service has accepted it while the envelope is still settling into the box.

Those are different situations. Giving all of them a spinning circle would tell the writer very little. Pretending every letter had left immediately would be worse: the desk would be making a promise it had not yet kept.

The bee gave us a way to show the difference within the world of the desk.

When the send is confirmed in time, the bee collects the letter on screen. The pickup has its own pace. A slow connection does not leave an envelope suspended in the air while an animation waits for permission to finish.

When confirmation takes longer, the mailbox accepts the letter and the person returns to the desk. The letter stays committed to being sent. There is no need to open it again, choose another stamp, or keep trying the send action.

The resting bee then explains what is happening.

A healthy send taking a little longer is greeted by a sleepy eye beginning to open. The courier is waking up to collect the mail. This detail mattered because we had briefly treated ordinary waiting as a problem. A substantial letter on a perfectly good connection could produce a “slow connection” note simply because it took longer than a small one.

That was the wrong explanation. The writer had done nothing wrong, and the connection had not necessarily failed. The bee waking gives that ordinary delay a place in the story.

If there is no connection, a small note hangs beneath the hive. It pictures the outgoing mailbox and says “no connection.” If a send has run into trouble or the wait has become too long, the note says “slow connection.”

The picture matters. It points back to the box that accepted the envelope: the letter is there, waiting for collection. The writer does not need to know what an upload is to understand where the letter went.

Even the note's timing needed care. If it appeared and vanished too quickly, the bee would fly off before anyone had time to understand the explanation. Once the note has opened, it stays long enough to be read.

When waiting mail is confirmed while the desk is visible, the bee makes the collection flight. If that happens while someone is elsewhere, the next view of the desk settles into the appropriate state. Nobody has to witness every animation for the objects to remain understandable.

There is an admin side to the story too. A letter that continues to have trouble appears in Unsent mail, where an admin can deal with it. The responsibility does not return as a mysterious draft among the writer's papers.

The distinction I kept coming back to was between putting a letter in the post and having it collected. The writer completes the first act. The courier represents the second.

That gives the bee something real to do. A small character, a mailbox and a hanging note carry a promise that a generic success animation could easily blur: the desk remembers the letter, and it tells the truth about the next part of its journey.
