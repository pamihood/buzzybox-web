About Postmello

Postmello is a quiet place for letters. Write and draw digital letters with friends and family. Texting can wait.

Kids can stay close to friends through words, drawings, and imagination, with room to think about what they want to say. With letters, staying in touch doesn’t have to mean being always available. Letters arrive digitally - and wait patiently.

Postmello is completely closed off. Only approved friends and family can exchange letters, from a desk or by email. Nothing public. No photo or video sharing. No forwarded memes.

Each person gets their very own desk, and collections make it a whole new world, from a fairy glen to a base on the moon, with matching mailboxes and stationery. Letters are written, drawn, or typed, and sent as a whole. No typing indicators, read receipts, online status, or push notifications. Just a mailbox flag when a letter arrives.

One desk, free. Room to grow. No ads or in-app currency.

Patrick Amihood, Founder

Patrick Amihood is the founder of Postmello and a dad of two in Palo Alto, California. Patrick spent nearly a decade at Google leading engineering teams, with a focus on human-computer interaction.

Trying to change texting goes back further. Before Google, Patrick co-founded ChatTag, a text messaging service where picking a tag created a room for everyone to chat as a group.

Patrick designed and built Postmello independently, for two daughters reaching the age when friends start texting. Roughly a hundred builds were shaped by those two daughters, their friends and early users.

A summer of letters, in Patrick’s words

I’ve spent my career in Silicon Valley building technology, and I know how much a device’s design shapes the way we use it. Now my own daughters are reaching the age when friends start texting.

Last summer, my older daughter, 9 years old, asked my wife to borrow our phone to text a best friend. It was a small request, and a reasonable one. I wanted another way to say yes.

I built the first version over the summer, and my older daughter began writing to her school friends, exchanging letters almost every other day. We traveled that summer, and she was able to keep in touch with her friends for those months we were abroad, and with the new friends she made along the way.

My younger daughter, 6 years old, likes to draw rainbows for her friends. A few words and a drawing arrive as a page made for one friend, instead of another message in a stream.

There’s a pleasure in it I remember from the old days: the postal ritual of choosing the paper, stamping the envelope, then waiting for the flag to go up when a reply comes back. Opening a letter someone made just for you never gets old.

At their age, I get to decide when they have the iPad. It’s a shared device at home, and I approve every friend they can write to. I’m comfortable with it because it’s completely closed off: nothing public, no photo or video sharing, no forwarded memes. The desk doesn’t follow them with push notifications.

They sit down at the desk, make something for a friend, send it, and get on with the day. The letter arrives, and the afternoon is still theirs. It’s a sense of connection without anxiety, and of anticipation without the constant pull to check.

I hope that this can help children retain their childhood for a little longer and postpone texting and social media.
